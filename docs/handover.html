Before anything else

Two things that are not website problems.

Both came out of talking to Marco. Neither is fixed by design, and both are worth more attention than everything below.

1. No contractor’s licence, no liability insurance

Marco has no C-33 Painting and Decorating licence and no liability cover. In California a contractor’s licence is required once labour and materials together reach $1,000 — raised from $500 on 1 January 2025 — so essentially every job he takes is over the line.

The exposure is not theoretical. Under Business & Professions Code §7031, an unlicensed contractor cannot sue to collect payment, however good the work and however clearly the money is owed. Worse, a client can sue to recover every dollar already paid — disgorgement applies even when the work was performed flawlessly. On a $6,000 restaurant job that is a $6,000 hole; across a run of commercial work it is ruinous.

Commercial and restaurant clients are also the ones most likely to ask for a licence number and a certificate of insurance before letting anyone on site — so this is blocking the exact work he says he is moving toward. A C-33 needs four years of journey-level experience in the last ten, two exams and a $25,000 bond. He has forty years of experience; the paperwork is the only obstacle.

Nothing on the new site claims a licence or insurance. That was deliberate. But no website can sell around this.

2. $500 a day is below the market

Marco prices in his head at $500 a day and quotes a single figure — the Peacock Entry was twelve days, so $6,000. The method is sound. The number looks low.

Generic specialty finish work in this market runs roughly $537–$963 a day, and trompe-l’œil and mural work is commonly quoted at $35–$150 per square foot. Marco is at the bottom of the range for ordinary faux finishing, while doing Italian-trained trompe-l’œil in Pacific Palisades, Beverly Hills and Malibu.

A painted loggia with pilasters, a landscape, a peacock and a painted dado, for $6,000, is not a competitive price. It is a signal that the buyer is getting something cheap. At the top of this trade the same room is quoted at several times that.

Keep quoting a fixed price per job — never publish or mention a day rate, because it invites an argument about days instead of a conversation about the work. Just raise the number behind it.

Read this first

The new site cannot be pasted into Wix.

This is the thing that decides everything else, so it is worth being blunt about. Wix has no way to upload a finished HTML site or edit the HTML of an existing one. Its embed tools add a box inside a Wix page; they do not replace the page.

Wix's own wording

“None of these tools allows you to access or change the HTML of your site.”

Wix Help Center — About Embeds and Codes on Your Site

An HTML embed also renders in a sandboxed iframe, so its text is not read as part of the page for search, and it does not resize cleanly on a phone. Dropping the whole site into one embed would look worse than what is there now.

So there are exactly two honest routes. Pick one before doing anything else.

Route A

Publishing the new site

About thirty minutes of work, then a wait for DNS. Nothing here costs money. Do not cancel the Wix plan until the last step — the old site should stay up until the new one is confirmed live.

  1. Get the files

    The finished site is the site/ folder in the repository, on the branch claude/marcolamberti-website-optimization-if7xd5. It contains index.html, an assets/ folder with the artwork, robots.txt and sitemap.xml. That folder is the entire website — there is nothing to install or compile.

  2. Put it on a host

    The simplest route needs no account setup or command line: open app.netlify.com/drop and drag the site folder onto the page. It publishes immediately at a temporary address like ml-art-9f2c.netlify.app.

    Check that address on a phone and a laptop before going any further. Cloudflare Pages and GitHub Pages work equally well if there is a preference.

  3. Note the site's Netlify name

    In Netlify, rename the site to something tidy (Site configuration → Change site name), for example marcolamberti, giving marcolamberti.netlify.app. The DNS step needs this exact name.

  4. Point the domain

    Wix runs the DNS for this domain and does not permit external nameservers, so the records are edited in place at Wix. In the Wix account: Domains → the Domain Actions icon next to marcolamberti.com → Manage DNS Records.

    TypeHostRemove (current)Add (new)

    Delete the old records after adding the new ones, or they will conflict. An apex domain cannot use a CNAME, which is why @ takes an A record and only www takes a CNAME.

    Email is safe: this domain currently has no MX records at all, so there is no mailbox to break. (Marco's address is a Yahoo one, which is a separate matter — see the last section.)

  5. Add the domain in Netlify and wait

    In Netlify: Domain management → Add a domain → marcolamberti.com. Netlify will show “Awaiting External DNS” until the records propagate — usually under an hour, occasionally up to 48. The HTTPS certificate is then issued automatically.

  6. Confirm, then cancel Wix

    Once https://marcolamberti.com and https://www.marcolamberti.com both load the new site with a padlock, the Wix Premium plan can be cancelled.

    Keep the domain registration. Cancelling the site plan is not the same as giving up the domain — check the renewal date and keep auto-renew on. Moving the registration to another registrar later is optional and independent of all of this.

Rolling it back

If anything goes wrong, put the three original A records and the cdn1.wixdns.net CNAME back exactly as they were in the table above. The Wix site returns once DNS propagates. Nothing in Route A deletes the Wix site itself.

Corrected biography Route B — item 09

Same facts as the current About page, with the grammar fixed and the celebrity client names removed — Marco has confirmed he has no permission to use them.

Marco Zanardi Lamberti was born in Mantova, a città d'arte in Lombardy, and grew up surrounded by architecture, painting and sculpture. He enrolled at art school in Verona, where he worked mainly in sculpture and his teachers took to calling him Maestro.

The third of four sons of a nobleman, he was expected to become an architect or an engineer. His father did not approve of art school. His mother did — and sent him south to stay with her brother on Ischia, in the bay of Naples, where he could keep studying. He took his degree there in fine arts.

He worked next as a painter-decorator and design consultant in Locarno and Paris. In the 1980s he moved to Los Angeles, which has been home ever since.

He has completed over 150 projects since — private homes, restaurants and commercial interiors — from Los Angeles down to San Diego. Alongside new work he restores existing murals and decorative finishes.
